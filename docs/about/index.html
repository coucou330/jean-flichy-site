Jean Flichy is a final-year student in the Bachelor of Global Engineering, a dual degree between CentraleSupélec and McGill University, specialising in electrical engineering. He is part of the programme's first cohort, co-leads McGill Robotics, and is looking for a master's programme in energy systems from September 2027.

I like problems where the physics, the schedule and the people all matter at once. So far that has meant modelling Québec's transmission grid, coordinating trades on an offshore converter station, testing weather forecasts for a cargo airship and designing a sailboat that steers itself. I learn best on concrete projects, close to the people who will use the result.

Why energy

My interest in energy started simply. A power cut at home made me realise how much I take electricity for granted. Reading Jean-Marc Jancovici and the Shift Project's plan for transforming the French economy showed me that the energy transition is a question of orders of magnitude, and I wanted to understand those numbers well enough to argue with them. Living on a boat or in the wild for a few weeks each year is a regular reminder that heat, water and power are finite.

What I enjoy most is the moment when an analysis has to become a decision, often before all the facts are in. I have found that pattern everywhere I have worked. At Flying Whales, forecast errors only mattered once they became a go/no-go rule. At Chantiers de l'Atlantique, a 1,250 MW platform was shaped as much by schedules and subcontractor choices as by its electrical design. In my capstone, an extreme Québec winter only becomes useful once it turns into a list of critical corridors.

I want to become an engineer who can take — and defend — the decisions that keep energy systems running when the weather turns.
