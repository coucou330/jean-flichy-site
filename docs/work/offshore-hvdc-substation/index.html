Offshore HVDC converter station

Planning and cross-trade coordination on a 1,250 MW platform for RTE

I spent the summer of 2026 on G36, one of two offshore converter stations Chantiers de l'Atlantique is building for RTE. My subjects cut across several trades, so nobody owned them: the weighing of the topside, the arrival plan for the barge, the full-scale mock-up and the replacement of a carcinogenic product. I coordinated them, and the industrial director adopted one of my planning options.

Context

G36 is a 1,250 MW high-voltage direct-current (HVDC) platform for RTE, the French transmission system operator. It will bring the power of the Centre Manche offshore wind farms ashore, and its topside alone weighs around 13,000 tonnes. The two stations are the largest of their kind built in France. Their contract is the largest in the history of a yard founded in 1861.

The outfitting team sits between the design office and the site. It reviews plans, writes procedures, builds schedules, orders material and supervises subcontractors, and it is organised by trade (HVAC, electrical, piping…). A subject that cuts across several trades falls inside none of them. Those subjects were mine.

What I did

Barge arrival

The lower part of the station is built in Poland and arrives by barge, and the plan allowed a single day between its arrival and laying the first block on top. I gathered every team working in that window and built a cross-trade Gantt chart. It showed that the sequence was not feasible: the scaffolding alone was more than the subcontractor could build in time. My supervisor, a colleague from the maritime department and I presented options to the industrial director, who adopted one of them.

Weighing the topside

Before the platform is lifted at sea, its weight and centre of gravity must be measured on 38 jacks. The specification had stalled since December. I went back through the first offers, consulted the structure and logistics teams, ran supplier meetings with a buyer and updated the specification. On my last day I presented it at the sourcing committee, in front of the project director. I also produced the end-of-outfitting date from which the project's backward schedule is built.

The mock-up and the smaller subjects

Every trade wanted to test something on the full-scale mock-up (cladding, insulation, cable trays, HVAC), and part of it was built for the client's review. I coordinated all the trades and subcontractors, booked the aerial platforms, wrote a daily report and ran a weekly meeting.

The product that protects bolted assemblies from rust is carcinogenic. I built a test bench comparing application methods and colours, got the trades to agree on one method and wrote the procedure. Nobody could prove where protection was needed at all, so I also wrote a two-year field test: protected and unprotected assemblies outdoors, under shelter and near grinding work, photographed every month.

For the installation subcontractor, I wrote a risk assessment and installation instructions for the large equipment, mostly HVAC.

Results

  • For the barge arrival, the industrial director adopted one of our options: bring the barge in earlier and move part of the work upstream, so that the industrial milestones hold.
  • The weighing specification was updated and presented to the sourcing committee.
  • The two-year field test I designed will settle, with evidence, a question nobody could answer before.
  • My procedures and instructions were issued in the project's controlled document system (reference, revision, writer, checker, approver).

What I learned

Almost every piece of information I needed already existed, usually in someone's head rather than in a document. A lot of my usefulness came from not letting a question drop and from walking to people's desks instead of sending a second email. My supervisor's most useful feedback was about large meetings: my job there is to orient the decision, so I should come with a recommendation and defend it. That is what I most want to keep working on.
